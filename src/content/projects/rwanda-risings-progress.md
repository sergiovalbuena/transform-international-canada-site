In 2009, I accepted an invitation from a Rwandan graduate student in Canada to “come and help” the mothers and grandmothers struggling to feed and house scores of orphans in his rural township. A few locals welcomed me and gathered widows and grandmothers from the hills. Their unified voice asked for new roofs, food and school fees for their kids languishing at home. I assembled a young team, and we sought a sustainable approach to the extensive needs.

We came up with a plan aimed at reducing health risks: new tin roofs so they have dry rooms to gather and sleep. Capture rainwater in a 100-litre barrel with a faucet. Replace the stone fire pit with an efficient, smokeless clay stove. Add a raised-bed garden for vegetables. Provide blankets, pots and a hoe. We could do it for $200, and we rehabilitated two homes each week for six weeks. My money was running short. I promised to fund the team to continue the work, but school fees would have to wait.

One week before I was due to leave, I visited a secondary boarding school at the outskirts of town. Students were away on holiday, but eight kids sitting on the steps stood up and made room. Inside, the headmaster explained that those kids were the top scorers in the National Exam at the end of 9th grade and qualified for secondary boarding school, which at that time was an amazing bargain at $250 a year. The kids on the steps, however, were too poor. Sometimes, at the last minute, a scholarship gets passed to the school, and they had been waiting there for days in hopes of getting one.

That night I called my wife, Toni, in Victoria. Toward the end of the call, I told her what I had seen.

“Well,” she said, “what are you going to do about it?”

“Nothing I can do. I’m here for the grandmothers. And I don’t have the money.”

“Just don’t plan on coming home until all those kids are enrolled. I’ll send the money,” she said.

That was it. The next day, I was there for grandmothers and for students. It has remained so ever since; and educating these determined kids has proven to make the biggest long-term gain in everything we do.

— John Jordan, Rwanda Rising founder
